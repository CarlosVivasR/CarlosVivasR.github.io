Research

Duplicated genes, and when the duplicate stops helping

Most human genes have at least one paralog — a duplicate copy retained in the genome after a duplication event. These copies have long been treated as functional backups. My PhD asks when that is actually true.

  1. 01

    Where the copy came from

    An entire genome doubled at once, or a single gene copied on its own. Same outcome, very different constraints.

  2. 02

    What time does to a pair

    Copies drift apart at some rate. Sequence identity is that drift — and it slows as changes pile onto changes.

  3. 03

    Two ways to ask if a gene matters

    Some people are healthy with both copies switched off. In culture, the same gene can be one a cell cannot live without.

  4. 04

    When the backup fails

    Most genes carry a paralog. Most inherited disease genes carry one that does not compensate. What separates them?

PhD project · ongoing

Paralog compensation in human health and disease — a computational approach integrating large-scale population genomics with evolutionary and expression data, supervised by Prof. Colm J. Ryan at the Conway Institute.

Vivas-Rodríguez, C., Matallanas, D., Ryan, C.J., McClean, S., Dennler, O. & Drabinska, J.

OrthoGather: a local platform for orthology-based proteome and proteomics comparisons and Gene Ontology enrichment

Oxford Bioinformatics — In revision

A locally hosted web application for comparative proteomic analysis. OrthoGather integrates orthology inference (via OrthoFinder) with Gene Ontology enrichment, enabling cross-species functional comparison without cloud dependency. It leverages annotations from any orthogroup member to bridge gaps in poorly annotated organisms.

"Duplication origin shapes paralog compensation in rare developmental disorders"

Poster on why losing some genes is harmless while losing others causes severe developmental disorders, and how a paralog's origin shapes that tolerance. Shared with the Genomics England community working in clinical and rare-disease genomics.

Oral talk · April 2026

CHAS Symposium 2026 — University College Dublin

"Duplication origin shapes loss-of-function tolerance in human paralog genes"

Why does the same kind of mutation cause severe disease in one gene while passing unnoticed in another? In this talk I argued that part of the answer lies in our paralogs — and, in particular, in how those duplications first arose. Being selected for an oral talk at the UCD College of Health and Agricultural Sciences symposium was a chance to share the work with researchers well outside my immediate field.

Flash talk · December 2025

"Natural Knock-Outs of human genes are more frequently observed in genes with paralogs"

Talk on how functional compensation between paralogs may contribute to the tolerance of loss-of-function genes in healthy human populations. The conference brought together researchers across genetics, evolutionary biology and bioinformatics.
