Overuse injuries in summer tournaments

A well-validated weak signal · Survival analysis on open data

Can information that is public before a tournament (workload, injury history, age, position, market value) tell us which players are more likely to suffer a severe overuse injury around it? Short answer: injury history predicts a little, and minutes played predict nothing.

Why

Summer tournaments come straight after a full club season, and “too many minutes” is the usual explanation when players break down. I wanted to see whether that holds up in open data, and to be clear about what open data can and cannot say about injury risk.

Data

  • Squads and club minutes: 15 tournaments from 2008 to 2025 (three World Cups, five Euros, six Copas América and the 2025 Club World Cup), 7,499 player-tournament entries. Sources: transfermarkt-datasets (CC0), the Fjelstul World Cup database (CC BY-SA 4.0) and Wikipedia squad lists.
  • Injuries: 31,528 records for 3,195 players, collected from public Transfermarkt pages. These have no open licence, so only aggregate results are shown here.
  • Outcome: the first severe overuse injury (29+ days out) between 30 days before kick-off and 14 days after the final. Of 427 injuries in those windows, 237 were overuse, 66 contact and 124 ambiguous; the ambiguous ones were excluded.

Method

A Cox proportional-hazards model (lifelines, ridge penalty 0.05), with one row per player per tournament and time counted from the start of the window. The primary model uses 14 covariates: age, market value, top-five league, club minutes in the previous 12 months, six injury-history features, days since the last injury, and position. It is fitted on 5,234 player-tournaments with 75 events.

I fixed this as the primary model and treat the other variants (by position, archetype and workload dimension) as exploratory. Then I validated it by leaving one tournament out: fit on 14 tournaments, score the 15th, repeat, and report the C-index with bootstrap confidence intervals.

Results

Covariate (primary model)Hazard ratio (95% CI)p
Recurrent injuries to the same body part1.17 (0.98–1.39)0.08
Prior severe injuries, 24 months1.10 (0.97–1.25)0.13
Club minutes, 12 months (per 1,000)1.00 (0.93–1.08)0.96
Goalkeeper vs defender0.85 (0.60–1.20)0.35
  • Validated C-index: 0.63 (95% CI 0.55–0.71), against 0.66 in-sample on the same player pairs. Some optimism, as expected, and the lower bound stays just above chance (0.5).
  • The model as a whole is not significant (likelihood-ratio test p ≈ 0.46), and results swing widely between tournaments because most have only a handful of events.

What I learned

  1. History predicts a little; workload predicts nothing. Recurrence is the strongest signal, but it doesn't reach p < 0.05. Club minutes sit at a hazard ratio of exactly 1.00 with a tight interval. That could mean workload doesn't matter, or that public data measures it badly.
  2. The goalkeeper “effect” was confounding. Without injury history, goalkeepers looked protected (HR 0.57, p = 0.08). With it, the effect shrinks to 0.85 (p = 0.35).
  3. Validate before you interpret. A C-index of 0.65 sounds respectable. Out of sample it is 0.63 and the overall test is null, so the honest headline is a weak signal and a clear negative result for workload, not a risk tool.

Limitations

  • 75 events for 14 covariates (about 5 per variable, below the usual 10), and no correction for the many model variants explored.
  • 124 ambiguous injuries were excluded; classifying them by hand could change the result.
  • 18 of the 75 events come from the 2025 Club World Cup.
  • No real exposure measure: tournament minutes are missing for most editions and club minutes are zero for 48% of players.
  • Transfermarkt records severe, high-profile injuries and star players better than minor injuries and squad players.
